## Summary

Roger Sherman (1721–1793) was born in Massachusetts and trained as a shoemaker and surveyor before becoming a lawyer, judge, and politician. By 1776, he was Connecticut's foremost delegate to the Continental Congress and among the most respected figures in American revolutionary politics. On June 10, 1776, he was appointed to the Committee of Five alongside [Thomas Jefferson]({{ '/news/people/thomas-jefferson-1776/' | relative_url }}), [John Adams]({{ '/news/people/john-adams-1776/' | relative_url }}), [Benjamin Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin-1776/' | relative_url }}), and Robert R. Livingston to draft the [Declaration of Independence]({{ '/news/politics/declaration-of-independence/' | relative_url }}). As one of the Declaration's signers on August 2, 1776, he committed his life and fortune to American independence at age 55—among the oldest signers.

Sherman's intellectual vision extended beyond the Declaration. He advocated during 1776–1777 discussions for a federal system that balanced state sovereignty with central authority, a position that would define the [Articles of Confederation]({{ '/news/politics/articles-of-confederation-1776/' | relative_url }}) and later the Constitution itself. His insistence on equal state representation and his skepticism of unchecked executive power influenced Connecticut's ratification debates and the 1787 Constitutional Convention, where Sherman played a decisive role as one of the Convention's elder statesmen.

## Early Life and Career

Roger Sherman was born on April 19, 1721, in Newton (now Newton Centre), Massachusetts, to William Sherman, a farmer and shoemaker, and Mehetabel Wellington. As one of seven children in a family of modest means, Sherman received only a basic common school education but supplemented it with independent reading in theology, law, and mathematics.

At age 22, following his father's death in 1743, Sherman relocated to New Milford, Connecticut, where his brother William had settled. He established himself as a shoemaker and surveyor, producing almanacs between 1750 and 1761 that demonstrated his mathematical aptitude and gained him local prominence. He read law under local attorneys and was admitted to the Connecticut bar in 1754, quickly building a practice that extended into merchant credit, real estate, and public finance.

His political career advanced in parallel: elected to the Connecticut General Assembly in 1755, appointed justice of the peace that same year, and elevated to judge of the Connecticut Superior Court in 1766—a position he held concurrently with his congressional duties until 1789. He married Elizabeth Hartwell in 1749 (who died in 1760, leaving him with seven children) and later married Rebecca Prescott in 1763, with whom he had eight more children. By the late 1760s Sherman was one of Connecticut's most prominent public figures, combining judicial authority, legislative experience, and mercantile acumen.

## The Committee of Five and the Declaration

On June 10, 1776, the Continental Congress appointed the Committee of Five to draft a formal declaration of independence. The five members were [Thomas Jefferson]({{ '/news/people/thomas-jefferson-1776/' | relative_url }}) (Virginia), [John Adams]({{ '/news/people/john-adams-1776/' | relative_url }}) (Massachusetts), [Benjamin Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin-1776/' | relative_url }}) (Pennsylvania), Robert R. Livingston (New York), and Roger Sherman (Connecticut). At 55, Sherman was the second oldest member after Franklin, then 70.

The committee assigned primary drafting responsibility to Jefferson, who composed his working draft between June 11 and June 28. Sherman's contributions to the final text cannot be isolated with certainty from the collaborative revision process, but his role was substantive: Adams later described Sherman as a man "never embarrassed or disheartened" in argument, one whose "judgment was excellent." The committee submitted its revised draft to Congress on June 28; Congress debated and amended the text on July 2, 3, and the morning of July 4.

The [Declaration of Independence]({{ '/news/politics/declaration-of-independence/' | relative_url }}) was formally adopted on July 4, 1776. Sherman's signature appeared on the engrossed parchment copy on August 2, 1776—the same day as most other signers. His previous signature on the Continental Association of 1774 had committed him to colonial resistance; the Declaration moved that commitment from boycott to outright independence.

## Federal Architecture: Articles of Confederation and the Connecticut Compromise

Sherman's contributions to 1776 extended to the structural debates over how the new nation would govern itself. In July and August 1776, John Dickinson led the drafting of the [Articles of Confederation]({{ '/news/politics/articles-of-confederation-1776/' | relative_url }}), the framework for a permanent union among the states. Sherman was a central participant in these debates, consistently advocating for equal state representation regardless of population—a position that favored smaller states like Connecticut over larger states like Virginia and Pennsylvania.

The debates of 1776 previewed the central conflict that would dominate the Constitutional Convention of 1787: whether representation should be proportional to population or equal per state. Sherman's position—equal representation in at least one legislative body—ultimately shaped the Connecticut Compromise (also called the Great Compromise or Sherman Compromise), which he formally proposed at the Constitutional Convention on June 11, 1787. Adopted by delegates on July 16, 1787, the compromise created a bicameral legislature with proportional representation in the House of Representatives and equal state representation in the Senate—the architecture that defines the United States Congress to the present day.

The breadth of Sherman's foundational involvement is unique in American history. He is the only person to have signed all four of the nation's major founding documents: the Continental Association (1774), the Declaration of Independence (1776), the Articles of Confederation (1781), and the Constitution (1787). No other Founder spans that full arc of the founding era.

## Significance

Sherman's central importance to 1776 lies in his ability to bridge practical statecraft with philosophical principle. While [Jefferson]({{ '/news/people/thomas-jefferson-1776/' | relative_url }}) articulated natural-rights ideology and [Adams]({{ '/news/people/john-adams-1776/' | relative_url }}) championed aggressive independence advocacy, Sherman provided the legal and structural grounding that transformed abstract principles into governance. His role on the Committee of Five placed him at the founding moment; his continued prominence through 1787 ensured conceptual consistency between the Declaration's promises and the Constitution's architecture.

Sherman's career also exemplified the revolutionary elite's diverse origins. Unlike Jefferson and [Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin-1776/' | relative_url }}), Sherman rose from artisanal roots—a shoemaker who became indispensable to nation-building. After ratification of the Constitution, he served as a U.S. Representative (1789–1791) and then as U.S. Senator from Connecticut (1791–1793). He died in office in New Haven on July 23, 1793, at age 72, having helped craft the Bill of Rights during his congressional tenure—his fourth and final foundational contribution to American governance.
