**Key figures**: Thomas Jefferson, John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Roger Sherman, Robert R. Livingston, George Wythe

## Summary

Thomas Jefferson entered 1776 as a 33-year-old Virginia lawyer and planter, known in Philadelphia primarily for his literary skill and his 1774 pamphlet *A Summary View of the Rights of British America*. By December he had authored the founding document of an independent nation and launched a sweeping revision of Virginia's legal code.

### Appointment to the Committee of Five

Jefferson had returned to Congress in May 1776, hoping still to return to Virginia to help shape his home state's new constitution. On June 11, 1776, Congress appointed him to the Committee of Five — alongside John Adams, [Benjamin Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin-1776/' | relative_url }}), Roger Sherman, and Robert R. Livingston — charged with drafting a formal statement of independence. Adams later recalled persuading the committee to assign the writing to Jefferson, citing Jefferson's reputation as a writer and the political wisdom of having a Virginian lead the effort. Jefferson, the youngest of the five at 33, accepted the assignment.

### Drafting the Declaration: June 11–28, 1776

Jefferson composed the draft largely alone over approximately seventeen days, working in a rented second-floor parlor in the Graff House at Seventh and Market Streets in Philadelphia. He drew on the Enlightenment natural-rights tradition — particularly John Locke's *Second Treatise of Government* — and on contemporary American texts, including George Mason's Virginia Declaration of Rights (adopted by the Virginia Convention on June 12, 1776) and a preamble Jefferson himself had written for a proposed Virginia constitution. Adams and [Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin-1776/' | relative_url }}) made minor revisions before the draft was submitted to Congress on June 28; Franklin's most celebrated alteration was reportedly changing "sacred and undeniable" truths to "self-evident" truths in the opening lines, though the precise attribution remains historically debated.

Congress debated the document on July 2–4 and made approximately eighty-six editorial changes, cutting roughly a quarter of Jefferson's text. The most consequential deletion was Jefferson's lengthy passage condemning the Atlantic slave trade as a moral wrong forced on the colonies by the British Crown — an argument that struck many delegates as both historically tendentious and politically untenable given South Carolina and Georgia's opposition. The omission was pointed: Jefferson at the time of drafting enslaved approximately 130 people at Monticello. The amended Declaration was adopted on July 4, 1776, and formally engrossed and signed by fifty-six delegates — including Franklin — on August 2, 1776.

### Virginia Constitutional Work and Legal Reform

Jefferson's revolutionary work in 1776 extended well beyond the Declaration. He had drafted three versions of a constitution for Virginia earlier that year; though they arrived in Williamsburg too late to be adopted in full, his preamble was attached verbatim to the constitution the Virginia Convention adopted in June. After leaving Congress in September 1776, Jefferson declined an appointment as a commissioner to France — a mission ultimately led by [Benjamin Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin-1776/' | relative_url }}) — and instead took a seat in the new Virginia House of Delegates. There he began coordinating a comprehensive revision of Virginia's legal code comprising 126 separate bills. Early priorities included the *Bill for Establishing Religious Freedom* (which became law in 1786) and the abolition of entail and primogeniture in land inheritance — legal changes that dismantled the formal basis of Virginia's hereditary planter aristocracy and which Jefferson later ranked among his three proudest lifetime achievements, alongside the Declaration and founding the University of Virginia.

## Significance

Jefferson's draftsmanship in 1776 gave the American Revolution its most enduring statement of purpose. The Declaration's assertion that "all men are created equal" and endowed with unalienable rights to "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness" distilled Enlightenment political philosophy into language that [Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin-1776/' | relative_url }}) would deploy diplomatically in the salons of Paris and that [Washington]({{ '/news/people/george-washington-1776/' | relative_url }})'s army would fight to make real on battlefields from Long Island to Trenton. The document would be invoked by abolitionists, suffragists, and civil rights movements long after Jefferson's death. The year established Jefferson as the foremost literary voice of the founding generation and launched a public career that would carry him to the governorship of Virginia, the office of secretary of state, and ultimately the presidency. His 1776 turn toward reforming Virginia's laws — particularly on religious liberty and entail — also marked the beginning of the domestic reform program he later regarded as among his proudest achievements, alongside the Declaration itself and the founding of the University of Virginia.

## Later Life & Legacy

The decades following 1776 confirmed Jefferson as the foremost intellectual architect of American independence and expanded his political influence across multiple domains of founding governance. After leaving the Continental Congress in September 1776, Jefferson pursued his domestic reform agenda in the Virginia House of Delegates through 1779, stewarding the *Bill for Establishing Religious Freedom* (passed in 1786) and abolishing entail — foundational legal changes that reflected the egalitarian principles he had written into the Declaration.

From 1779 to 1781, Jefferson served as Governor of Virginia, a tenure marked by both administrative challenges and military crisis. The state faced British invasion under General William Phillips and Lord Cornwallis in 1780–1781; under intense pressure and pursued as a target of capture, Jefferson evacuated the state capital from Richmond and retreated to Monticello, where he narrowly escaped a British cavalry raid. Though his governorship ended under a cloud of controversy, the experience deepened his understanding of executive limits and state power. The death of his wife Martha in September 1782 drove Jefferson into a period of grief and seclusion at Monticello, where he began writing *Notes on the State of Virginia*, a philosophical and descriptive work that would become one of the founding era's most influential statements of American geography, natural history, and political economy.

In 1784, Congress appointed Jefferson as a commissioner to Paris to negotiate post-war commercial treaties, succeeding Benjamin Franklin in the diplomatic mission. He remained in France as Minister (1785–1789), a formative period during which he immersed himself in French Enlightenment society, corresponded with philosophes, and represented American interests in the post-war reconstruction of European commerce and politics. His time in France refined his political thinking, exposing him to radical egalitarian ideas (notably the French Revolution, whose early phases he witnessed) while also tempering his view of pure democracy. On his return to America in 1790, President George Washington appointed him Secretary of State, launching the final phase of his public career.

Jefferson's arc from Declaration author to statesman, diplomat, and executive illustrates how the intellectual commitments of 1776 translated into a lifetime of founding-era political practice. His presidency (1801–1809) would include the Louisiana Purchase and the expedition of Lewis and Clark — expansions of the continental republic that fulfilled in geographical and demographic terms the revolutionary vision of 1776. He died on July 4, 1826, fifty years to the day after the Declaration's adoption, an ending that later generations recognized as symbolic of his inseparability from the founding document.
