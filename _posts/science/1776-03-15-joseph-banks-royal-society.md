# Sir Joseph Banks and the Royal Society in 1776

**Category**: Science & Discovery

**Key figures**: Joseph Banks, Captain James Cook, King George III, Hans Sloane, Daniel Solander, Benjamin Franklin

## Summary

Sir Joseph Banks (1743–1820), baronet naturalist and one of the towering figures of Enlightenment science, reached a watershed moment in his career in 1776. Recently returned from Captain Cook's first Pacific voyage aboard HMS *Endeavour* (1768–1771), where he had served as chief naturalist and collected thousands of specimens, Banks was establishing himself as Britain's premier man of science and facilitator of international scientific exchange. In 1776, at age 32, Banks was transitioning from field naturalist to institutional leader, leveraging his wealth, scientific credentials, and royal connections to influence the Royal Society and the course of global science during an age of revolutionary upheaval.

In 1776, Banks held significant informal influence within British science, though he had not yet assumed the presidency of the Royal Society (which he would hold from 1778 to 1820, a tenure of 41 years — the longest in the Society's history). His 1771 return from the Pacific had sparked intense demand for his specimens, drawings, and journals; the British Museum and private collectors vied for access to the thousands of insects, plants, birds, and zoological specimens he had collected. Banks and his team had collected approximately 30,000 plant, animal, and mineral specimens from the Pacific, among them some 1,400 species entirely new to European science. More importantly, his published account of the voyage — detailed, scientifically rigorous, and accessible — made him a celebrity within European scientific circles.

## 1776: A Year of Transition

Banks's 1776 activities fell into four overlapping domains: active natural history research, specimen curation and exchange, preparation for Cook's third voyage, and cultivation of scientific and royal patronage.

### The Herbarium and Specimen Exchange

At his Soho Square residence in London — later famous as one of the great scientific salons of the age — Banks in 1776 was actively cataloguing and distributing specimens from the *Endeavour* voyage. His collections occupied an entire wing and were tended by Swedish naturalist Daniel Solander (1733–1782), who served as Banks's librarian and taxonomic collaborator. The two men corresponded with the Académie des Sciences in Paris, the Swedish Academy of Sciences (through Solander's connections to Carl Linnaeus), and leading naturalists across Europe, building networks of specimen exchange and collaborative research that would define Banks's role as scientific statesman.

By 1776, Banks had commissioned Sydney Parkinson's botanical illustrations — 743 drawings of Pacific plants — to be engraved for publication. This planned *Florilegium* (ultimately never completed in Banks's lifetime) became one of the great unfinished projects of Enlightenment science; the engravings were finally printed by the Natural History Museum London in 34 volumes between 1980 and 1990.

### Banks and Cook's Third Voyage

The most significant scientific event Banks was involved with in 1776 was the departure of [Captain Cook's third voyage]({{ '/news/science/captain-cook-third-voyage/' | relative_url }}). The expedition — HMS *Resolution* (462 tons, Captain Cook) and HMS *Discovery* (298 tons, Captain Charles Clerke) — departed Plymouth on July 12, 1776, tasked with locating the Northwest Passage from the Pacific side and, as a secondary mission, returning the Tahitian navigator Omai to his home island.

Banks had hoped to accompany Cook's second voyage (1772–1775) but withdrew after a dispute with the Admiralty over the structural modifications needed to accommodate his enlarged scientific party. For the third voyage, Banks played a different role: he remained in London as the expedition's scientific patron and intellectual sponsor, ensuring that the voyage carried state-of-the-art instruments, including the Kendall K1 chronometer — a copy of [John Harrison's H4]({{ '/news/science/john-harrison-marine-chronometer/' | relative_url }}) — which enabled precise longitude determination and transformed maritime navigation. Banks also helped select William Anderson as the voyage's surgeon-naturalist, through whom he expected detailed botanical and zoological observations.

Banks corresponded regularly with Cook and Cook's officers throughout the third voyage's planning stages in early 1776, and his influence on the scientific instrumentation and objectives was considerable, even from shore.

### Franklin, Science, and the Revolution

The American Revolution paradoxically elevated Banks's stature in British science. As traditional political institutions fractured and military campaigns consumed resources, the Royal Society — insulated from direct political control — became an arena where Britain maintained its intellectual standing.

Banks's relationship with [Benjamin Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin-1776/' | relative_url }}) exemplified the "Republic of Letters" ideal. Franklin, the American scientist-diplomat who had corresponded with the Royal Society for decades, was formally elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1756 and had long exchanged scientific observations with British counterparts. In 1776, as Franklin departed for France as America's diplomatic representative, his scientific correspondence — including exchanges with Banks — continued across political battle lines. Banks reportedly helped secure a provision that American privateers should not interfere with the scientific vessels of Cook's third voyage; Franklin issued similar instructions to American captains from Paris, acknowledging that scientific exploration transcended national conflict.

This mutual commitment to scientific internationalism during wartime established a precedent that would be invoked repeatedly in subsequent conflicts. When France and Britain went to war after the French treaty with America (February 1778), Banks and his continental counterparts maintained specimen exchange and published each other's findings in their respective national journals.

### Royal Connections and Kew Gardens

Banks's access to George III was an asset he cultivated actively in 1776. The king, who shared Banks's passion for botany and agriculture, welcomed Banks's visits to Kew Gardens — then a royal pleasure ground that Banks was systematically transforming into a serious botanical research institution. Banks served as de facto director of Kew from around 1773 without any formal title, advising on plant acquisitions, directing plant hunters to collect specimens from the Pacific, India, and the Americas, and curating the growing living collection.

By 1776, Kew housed specimens from five continents, and Banks was corresponding with colonial governors and ship captains to expand the collection further. His vision was that Kew should function as an imperial botanical clearinghouse — identifying economically valuable plants worldwide, propagating them at Kew, and then distributing them to suitable climates in the British Empire. This vision would eventually produce the transfer of breadfruit from Tahiti to the Caribbean (via the Bounty voyage, 1789), the cultivation of tea in India, and the transplantation of rubber to Southeast Asia — projects that reshaped global agriculture.

## Significance

Banks's role in 1776 was consequential in five dimensions:

1. **Advancing Expedition Science** — Banks's curation of Cook's specimens and his detailed documentation of natural history from the South Pacific established protocols for collecting, preserving, and scientifically analyzing biological specimens that influenced natural history practice for generations. His methodology elevated taxonomy from cataloguing to comparative science.

2. **Institutional Leadership** — Though not yet president of the Royal Society in 1776, Banks's influence over the Society's agenda was profound. He advocated for funding [Cook's third voyage]({{ '/news/science/captain-cook-third-voyage/' | relative_url }}) (departing July 12, 1776), shaped priorities for overseas exploration, and used the Society's credibility to advance his own research agenda. His election to the Royal Society Presidency in November 1778 crowned this rising influence.

3. **Maintaining Scientific Internationalism** — During the American Revolution, Banks's correspondence with Franklin and other American naturalists kept scientific collaboration alive across political divisions. His later presidency of the Royal Society would institutionalize this commitment to transnational science. The Royal Society maintained exchanges with the Académie des Sciences even during Britain's war with France after 1778.

4. **Patronage of Global Exploration** — Banks's vision of using scientific expeditions to map, classify, and understand the natural world profoundly shaped British imperial policy and the relationship between science and colonialism. His 1776-era preparations for Cook's third voyage — which would ultimately reach the Hawaiian Islands (first European contact, January 1778) and the North American Pacific coast — exemplified how science served both knowledge and imperial strategy.

5. **Kew Gardens as Scientific Institution** — Banks's 1776 activities at Kew laid the groundwork for its transformation from royal garden to global botanical exchange. The plants that transited Kew during his informal tenure would eventually include species that transformed British colonial agriculture across three continents.

## The Royal Society in 1776

In 1776, the Royal Society of London — founded in 1660 under Charles II — was governed by Sir John Pringle (president 1772–1778), a Scottish physician and Banks's ally. The Society met weekly during the London season at its rooms in Crane Court (it would not move to Somerset House until 1780) and published its *Philosophical Transactions*, which in 1776 included papers on astronomy, electricity, natural history, and medicine. The *Transactions* remained one of the few journals in which British and continental scientists published side by side during the Revolutionary War, maintaining scientific dialogue while governments traded cannon fire.

Banks attended Society meetings regularly in 1776, presenting specimens and observations, proposing new Fellows, and building the coalition of supporters who would elect him President in 1778. Among those elected Fellows in or around 1776 was Alessandro Volta, the Italian physicist whose work on electrical batteries would eventually produce the voltaic pile (1799) — and who, like Banks, exemplified the transnational character of Enlightenment science.
