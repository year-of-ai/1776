# Joseph Priestley and Pneumatic Chemistry in 1776

**Category**: Science & Discovery

**Key figures**: Joseph Priestley, Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier, Carl Wilhelm Scheele, Georg Ernst Stahl, Benjamin Franklin

## Summary

Joseph Priestley (1733–1804), English Unitarian minister and natural philosopher, was one of the leading experimental chemists of the 18th-century Scientific Revolution. In 1776, Priestley was actively extending and publishing his pneumatic chemistry research — focused on isolating and characterizing different "airs" (gases) — building on his breakthrough discovery of August 1, 1774, when he had first isolated "dephlogisticated air" at Bowood House in Wiltshire. By 1776, Priestley was disseminating his findings through publications, correspondence, and repeated experiments that would ultimately provide the empirical foundation for the oxygen theory of combustion.

## Background: The 1774 Discovery

The discovery that Priestley's 1776 work elaborated originated on August 1, 1774, at Bowood House — the Wiltshire country estate of his patron, William Petty, 2nd Earl of Shelburne. Using a 12-inch (30 cm) burning lens to focus sunlight onto a sample of mercury oxide (*mercurius calcinatus per se*) sealed in a glass vessel, Priestley collected the gas released into an inverted tube suspended over mercury. The colorless gas had astonishing properties: a glowing splint thrust into it burst into vigorous flame, and mice confined in it survived approximately four times as long as mice in ordinary air before succumbing.

Priestley initially called the gas "dephlogisticated air" — reasoning within the then-dominant phlogiston theory that he had isolated air from which all phlogiston had been removed, making it maximally capable of absorbing the phlogiston released during combustion and respiration. This name reflected Priestley's theoretical framework rather than the gas's actual properties. He described the discovery's subjective impact in his own words: "I cannot at this time help recollecting a scene which must have excited in me the most extravagant expectations... the candle burned in this air with a remarkably vigorous flame."

## 1776: Publication, Experimentation, and Continental Exchange

In 1776, Priestley was active on three fronts: continuing his experimental investigations, preparing the third volume of his major series, and transmitting his findings across Europe.

### Publication of Volume II and Preparation of Volume III

Priestley's foundational work appeared as *Experiments and Observations on Different Kinds of Air* in three volumes (1774, 1775, 1777) plus additional supplementary volumes thereafter. Volume II (1775) had described the dephlogisticated air discovery for print audiences. By 1776, Priestley was conducting follow-up experiments intended for Volume III (1777), varying experimental conditions — different metal oxides (lead oxide, manganese dioxide), different collection vessels, different illumination sources — to characterize the gas more fully and extend his pneumatic chemistry to other substances.

His 1776 experiments included work on "nitrous air" (nitric oxide, NO), "fixed air" (carbon dioxide, CO₂), and "inflammable air" (hydrogen, H₂), all of which he treated as distinct "airs" with specific chemical properties. This systematic approach to cataloguing gaseous substances represented a new paradigm in chemistry: gases were not just modified atmospheric air but discrete chemical entities with reproducible properties.

### The Paris Visit and Lavoisier's Response

The critical transmission of Priestley's dephlogisticated air discovery to continental Europe occurred in October 1774, when Priestley traveled to Paris as part of Shelburne's diplomatic entourage and described his mercury oxide experiments at a dinner attended by [Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier]({{ '/news/science/lavoisier-combustion-1776/' | relative_url }}) and other members of the Académie des Sciences. Lavoisier recorded this conversation in his laboratory notebooks.

By 1776, Lavoisier had repeated Priestley's experiments with far greater quantitative precision, meticulously weighing the mercury oxide before and after heating and measuring the volume of gas produced. His measurements showed that the weight of mercury oxide before heating equaled the weight of mercury produced plus the weight of the gas collected — a conservation of mass relationship that would become the cornerstone of modern chemistry. Lavoisier's *Mémoire sur la nature du principe qui se combine avec les métaux pendant leur calcination* (read to the Académie in April 1775, published 1778) reinterpreted Priestley's dephlogisticated air as a distinct elemental substance that combined with metals during calcination. Lavoisier would formally name it "oxygen" (from Greek *oxys*, acid, + *genes*, forming) in his 1779 *Considérations générales sur la nature des acides*.

Priestley and Lavoisier maintained a scientific correspondence during this period, and Priestley's published volumes circulated widely in France. The relationship was simultaneously collaborative and competitive: both men were advancing the frontier of pneumatic chemistry, but they reached opposite theoretical conclusions from the same experimental evidence.

### Priestley and Franklin: Science Across the Revolution

Priestley's connection to [Benjamin Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin-1776/' | relative_url }}) was deep and long-standing. Franklin had encouraged Priestley's chemistry career from its earliest stages, and Priestley dedicated his 1767 *History and Present State of Electricity* to Franklin. In 1776, as Franklin sailed for France to serve as American diplomatic commissioner, scientific correspondence between the two men continued across Atlantic political divisions. Franklin's letters to Priestley from this period — preserved in the American Philosophical Society archives — discuss both political developments and natural philosophy.

This connection exemplified the "Republic of Letters" — the informal transnational network of Enlightenment scholars who maintained dialogue across national borders during the American Revolution. While [King George III]({{ '/news/people/king-george-iii-1776/' | relative_url }}) and the Continental Congress traded cannon fire and declarations, Priestley, Franklin, Lavoisier, Volta, and Banks exchanged specimens, papers, and instruments across the same political battle lines. Scientific internationalism was not incidental to the Enlightenment — it was one of its core commitments.

### Priestley's Dissenting Political Context

In 1776, Priestley was not merely a chemist but a prominent Dissenting minister (he led a congregation at Mill Hill in Leeds until 1773, then moved to Birmingham) and political pamphleteer. He was deeply sympathetic to the American cause, and his political writings — including *An Essay on the First Principles of Government* (1768) — articulated a theory of natural rights that influenced the intellectual milieu in which Thomas Jefferson wrote the Declaration of Independence. Jeremy Bentham acknowledged Priestley's influence on his formulation of the principle of utility.

This political engagement placed Priestley in a dangerous position in the 1780s and 1790s, when his support for the French Revolution led a Birmingham mob — inflamed by church-and-king sentiment — to destroy his house, laboratory, and library in the "Priestley Riots" of July 14, 1791 (the second anniversary of the fall of the Bastille). Priestley emigrated to Pennsylvania in 1794, where he lived until his death in 1804, never abandoning the phlogiston theory despite Lavoisier's definitive arguments.

## The Phlogiston Paradox

The most striking feature of Priestley's scientific career — nowhere more evident than in his 1776 work — is what historians of science call the "phlogiston paradox." Priestley possessed the experimental data, the instrumental sophistication, and the intellectual rigor to have discovered the oxygen theory of combustion himself, yet he never did. He collected dephlogisticated air, measured its superior support for combustion and respiration, noted its production from metal oxides, and characterized its physical properties with precision — but he never abandoned the theoretical framework that blocked him from interpreting these observations correctly.

The philosopher of science Thomas Kuhn cited Priestley's case in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) as a paradigmatic example of how theoretical commitments shape experimental interpretation: Priestley could not see what the evidence showed because his phlogiston framework organized his perceptions in advance. Lavoisier, working from a different theoretical starting point and with the advantage of quantitative mass measurements, made the interpretive leap that Priestley's prior theory prevented.

## Significance

Priestley's 1776 experiments and publications rank among the most consequential in the history of chemistry:

1. **Isolation and Characterization of Multiple Gases** — Before Priestley's systematic program, chemists worked with ill-defined "airs" of uncertain composition. His experimental rigor in isolating chemically pure gases with reproducible properties established the methodology of pneumatic chemistry, identifying at least eight distinct gases (nitric oxide, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, hydrogen chloride, ammonia, sulfur dioxide, and others) in addition to oxygen.

2. **Paradox of Discovery** — Priestley discovered oxygen while working within the phlogiston paradigm; ironically, his discovery supplied the empirical evidence that would refute the theory to which he remained loyal until his death in 1804. Few scientists have made discoveries so fundamental to the overthrow of their own theoretical framework.

3. **Foundation of the Chemical Revolution** — The identification of dephlogisticated air, combined with [Lavoisier's quantitative experiments]({{ '/news/science/lavoisier-combustion-1776/' | relative_url }}) and precise mass measurements, enabled the transition from phlogiston mysticism to modern analytical chemistry grounded in elemental composition and atomic theory.

4. **Instrumentation and Method** — Priestley's use of the burning lens and his systematic variation of experimental conditions (different metal oxides, different reaction vessels, different collection methods) demonstrated a level of experimental sophistication that influenced later chemists and established best practices in laboratory science.

5. **International Scientific Community** — Priestley's work, disseminated through his published *Experiments and Observations on Different Kinds of Air* and his correspondence with Lavoisier, Carl Wilhelm Scheele (who had also independently isolated oxygen around 1771–1772 but published later, in 1777), and Franklin, exemplified how experimental discovery transcended national boundaries even during political upheaval. The American Revolution was underway as Priestley conducted his experiments in England; Franklin was crossing the Atlantic to Paris; and Lavoisier was replicating Priestley's experiments in his Paris laboratory.

6. **Connection to Alessandro Volta** — Priestley's pneumatic chemistry ran in parallel with other gas-related discoveries of the era. [Alessandro Volta]({{ '/news/science/volta-methane-1776/' | relative_url }}), working in Como in 1776, isolated and characterized methane ("inflammable air from marshes") — another gas with specific chemical properties, and another step in the systematic mapping of the gaseous world that Priestley had pioneered.
