## Summary

On May 20, 1776, Mecklenburg County, North Carolina—a county encompassing present-day Charlotte and surrounding territory—convened and adopted a declaration asserting the dissolution of all political connection to Great Britain and the Crown. The Mecklenburg Declaration consisted of twenty resolutions rejecting royal authority, asserting local sovereignty, and calling for the establishment of a provisional government based on republican principles.

The resolves denied the authority of Parliament and Crown, declared all prior laws and appointments made under royal authority null and void, established a County Congress to serve as a provisional government, and pledged support for the cause of American independence. The declaration was dispatched to the Continental Congress in Philadelphia and published in the *Cape Fear Mercury* and other colonial newspapers in June 1776.

The Mecklenburg Declaration arrived 45 days before the Continental Congress adopted the federal [Declaration of Independence]({{ '/news/politics/declaration-of-independence/' | relative_url }}) on July 4, 1776. North Carolina had already passed the [Halifax Resolves]({{ '/news/politics/halifax-resolves-1776/' | relative_url }}) on April 12, 1776, which authorized its Congressional delegates to vote for independence—making the colony one of the earliest drivers of independence sentiment at the continental level.

## Historical Background

Mecklenburg County, established in 1762 and named for the German birthplace of Queen Charlotte (wife of King George III), was a backcountry region settled predominantly by Scots-Irish Presbyterians, Welsh Baptists, and German Reformed congregations. This population had limited loyalty to the British Crown, strong Calvinist theological commitments to covenant theology and resistance to tyranny, and acute resentment of colonial taxation policy. By 1775, Mecklenburg was home to a vigorous Committee of Safety, a local governing body established by the Continental Congress to coordinate resistance.

The convening of May 1776 took place against an accelerating backdrop of military and political crisis. The [Battle of Moore's Creek Bridge]({{ '/news/military/battle-of-moores-creek-bridge-1776/' | relative_url }}) on February 27, 1776 had suppressed Loyalist resistance in North Carolina, destroying a Highland Scottish force assembled under Donald MacDonald that had planned to march to the coast to rendezvous with British regulars. North Carolina's Royal Governor, Josiah Martin, had fled to a British warship in May 1775, effectively ending royal civil government in the colony. Thomas Paine's *Common Sense*, published on January 10, 1776, had circulated widely through the Carolina backcountry and the [Halifax Resolves]({{ '/news/politics/halifax-resolves-1776/' | relative_url }}) of April 12 had made North Carolina the first colony to formally authorize its delegates to vote for independence at Philadelphia.

## The May 19–20 Meeting

Colonel Thomas Polk, commander of the Mecklenburg County militia and a kinsman of future President James K. Polk (1845–1849), called county delegates together for a general meeting at the Charlotte Court House. Twenty-seven representatives from the county's militia companies and townships assembled on May 19–20, 1776. Abraham Alexander was elected chairman of the proceedings; John McKnitt Alexander served as secretary and was responsible for recording the resolutions in the official minutes.

The assembled delegates drafted and approved twenty resolutions. The key provisions included:

- **Dissolution of British authority**: All commissions granted by the Crown or Parliament were declared null and void; all officers holding royal commissions were ordered to cease exercising that authority.
- **Provisional self-government**: A County Congress was established with authority to govern Mecklenburg until a permanent government was constituted at the provincial level.
- **Military readiness**: The resolutions called for the organization and readiness of Mecklenburg's militia and for support of Continental forces.
- **Repudiation of reconciliation**: The document explicitly rejected the possibility of reconciliation on existing terms, distinguishing Mecklenburg's position from more moderate colonial assemblies still seeking negotiated settlement.

Colonel Polk is said to have forwarded the resolutions by express rider toward Philadelphia, though documentary evidence of congressional receipt has not been established.

## The Authenticity Debate

The Mecklenburg Declaration's historical standing has been contested for over two centuries, centering on the loss of the original documents.

The primary manuscript—the secretary's copy of the twenty resolutions—was reportedly destroyed in a house fire at the home of John McKnitt Alexander in April 1800. The surviving accounts rest on three sources: (1) a reconstruction from memory by John McKnitt Alexander's son, Jack McKnitt Alexander, composed after 1800; (2) newspaper accounts, particularly a partial reprint in the *Raleigh Register* in 1819 purportedly reproducing the original *Cape Fear Mercury* edition of June 1776; and (3) testimony from surviving participants collected decades after the event.

John Adams read the 1819 *Raleigh Register* account and wrote to [Thomas Jefferson]({{ '/news/people/thomas-jefferson-1776/' | relative_url }}) asking, "How do you explain it?" Jefferson responded skeptically, suggesting the document might be a later fabrication shaped by the federal Declaration's language—phrasing he doubted could have appeared independently in May 1776. This Jefferson–Adams exchange sharpened scholarly scrutiny. Some historians argue that the reconstructed May 20 text reflects anachronistic phrasing borrowed from the July 4 Declaration, and is therefore a later confabulation rather than a genuine contemporaneous document.

A separate document, the **Mecklenburg Resolves of May 31, 1776**—less sweeping in character and focused on local governance rather than outright independence—survives in authenticated form and is undisputed by scholars. Some historians argue this May 31 document is the actual declaration adopted at the meeting, with the May 20 date and independence language added by later memory.

North Carolina has traditionally celebrated May 20 as Mecklenburg Day. The date appears on the North Carolina state flag and state seal—inscribed as a matter of state pride—as "May 20, 1775," reflecting a separate local controversy over an earlier set of county resolves.

## Significance

The Mecklenburg Declaration holds complex historical standing. While celebrated in North Carolina as a precursor to national independence, its authenticity remains disputed; the document survives primarily through later newspaper accounts rather than authenticated originals. Nevertheless—whether or not the May 20 text is fully accurate—the episode demonstrates that independence sentiment was generating formal local assertions well before Philadelphia acted.

The declaration's significance is both symbolic and substantive. Symbolically, it illustrates that independence was not a top-down act imposed by the Continental Congress but rather a cascading grassroots movement across multiple scales of governance: from county (Mecklenburg, May 1776) to colony (the [Halifax Resolves]({{ '/news/politics/halifax-resolves-1776/' | relative_url }}), April 1776) to the Continental level (July 4, 1776). Substantively, the episode reveals how the Scots-Irish backcountry—far from the urban centers of Boston, Philadelphia, and Williamsburg—independently arrived at radical positions that mirrored and sometimes anticipated the formal declarations of the founding.

The Mecklenburg Declaration also stands in a lineage of progressive state-level assertions in spring 1776, alongside Virginia's [Declaration of Rights]({{ '/news/politics/virginia-declaration-of-rights/' | relative_url }}) (June 12, 1776) and the [Lee Resolution]({{ '/news/politics/lee-resolution/' | relative_url }}) (June 7–July 2, 1776), each of which built toward the formal moment of July 4. Taken together, these local and colonial declarations demonstrate that American independence was a process, not a single event, and that its intellectual and political groundwork was laid county by county and colony by colony throughout the first half of 1776.
