## Summary

The American Revolution of 1776 was as much a religious as a political transformation. Throughout the colonies, ministers framed the struggle for independence in theological terms: the Continental Congress itself opened with prayer, and clergy across denominations cast the Revolution as a defense of God-given rights against tyranny. In 1776 specifically, clerical voices crystallized a vision of independence rooted in Calvinist providentialism, natural-law morality derived from Christian teaching, and the conviction that the colonies' collective resistance to "enslaving" British authority reflected divine will. Major theological figures like **John Witherspoon**, the Scottish-trained Presbyterian minister and New Jersey delegate to Congress (the only clergyman to sign the [Declaration of Independence](/news/politics/declaration-of-independence/)), wove Reformed theology into the language of natural rights, arguing that the colonists' cause was sanctioned by God. Similarly, **Samuel Hopkins**, the New England Congregationalist, transformed the antislavery question—glaring in a Declaration asserting universal liberty—into a religious imperative demanding the young republic confront slavery as a sin.

The religious dimensions of 1776 were not merely rhetorical. Clergy served as military chaplains, maintained morale through sermons, and in many cases risked property and life for independence. Yet the year also exposed tensions: Anglican clergy split between patriots and loyalists; the Continental Congress's prayer still reflects the uneasy inclusion of multiple Christian traditions under one revolutionary banner; and the promise that all men are "created equal" by "their Creator" sits in unresolved tension with slavery and the subordination of women—contradictions rooted partly in competing theological traditions even among the Founders.

## The Theological Foundation of Revolutionary Argument

### Natural Rights and the "Creator"

The most famous religious resonance of 1776 is the Declaration of Independence's assertion that men are "endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights." This language was not merely rhetorical decoration: it embedded the revolutionary argument within a theological framework that made rights transcendent and pre-political, beyond Parliament's reach because they originated with God rather than human law. Thomas Jefferson, who drafted the Declaration, drew on both Locke's natural-rights philosophy and the deist conviction that a rational Creator had designed the universe with moral laws built in. The [Declaration of Independence](/news/politics/declaration-of-independence/) was thus simultaneously a political document and a theological claim about the nature of human dignity.

The [Virginia Declaration of Rights](/news/politics/virginia-declaration-of-rights/), drafted by George Mason and adopted on June 12, 1776—three weeks before the Continental Declaration—explicitly declared "that all men are by nature equally free and independent, and have certain inherent rights." Mason's document also guaranteed "the free exercise of religion," making Virginia's founding text one of the first legal guarantees of religious freedom in American history.

### Calvinist Providentialism

New England Congregationalists and Presbyterians throughout the mid-Atlantic brought a specifically Calvinist theological framework to the Revolution: the conviction that God governed history through providential action, rewarding righteousness and punishing sin at the level of nations. This "federal theology" drew on the biblical covenant tradition, treating the American colonies as a new Israel bound in covenant with God, whose collective virtue or vice would determine God's favor in the conflict with Britain. Ministerial "election sermons" and "fast day sermons"—a colonial tradition in which clergy preached on the state of the public during official days of fasting or prayer—became vehicles for political-theological analysis of the Revolution.

In 1776, the Continental Congress proclaimed multiple fast days; on July 20, Congress called a national day of humiliation, fasting, and prayer. These were not merely ceremonial: they reflected a genuine belief that God's favor was conditional on moral behavior, including resistance to tyranny.

## Key Religious Figures of 1776

### John Witherspoon: Theologian-Statesman

John Witherspoon (1723–1794) was born in Scotland, educated at the University of Edinburgh, and arrived in America in 1768 to become president of the College of New Jersey (later Princeton University). He was the only clergyman among the signers of the Declaration of Independence. In 1776, Witherspoon delivered "The Dominion of Providence over the Passions of Men" (May 17, 1776), a sermon preaching that God's sovereignty extended to political events and that colonial resistance to Britain was morally justified under Calvinist political theology. He argued that passive submission to tyranny was itself sinful—a direct religious sanction for revolution. Witherspoon then took his seat in the Continental Congress, voted for independence on July 2, 1776, and signed the Declaration on August 2.

Witherspoon's influence extended beyond his own writing: as president of Princeton, he had educated many future Founders, including James Madison (class of 1771), Philip Freneau, Aaron Burr, and Henry Lee. His fusion of Scottish Common Sense philosophy with Reformed theology created an intellectual framework that shaped American religious and political thought for a generation.

### Samuel Hopkins: Religion and Antislavery

Samuel Hopkins (1721–1803), a Congregationalist minister in Newport, Rhode Island, represents 1776's most theologically radical engagement with the Revolution's contradictions. Hopkins was a student of Jonathan Edwards and a leader of the "New Divinity" movement in New England theology. In 1776, Newport was a major center of the slave trade, and Hopkins had become an outspoken abolitionist on explicitly theological grounds: slavery was a sin, and a republic founded on liberty while practicing slavery was inviting divine judgment. His 1776 antislavery petition to the Continental Congress—signed with fellow minister Ezra Stiles—argued that the colonies could not expect God's favor in the war if they maintained the slave trade.

Hopkins's position was prophetic and largely unheeded in 1776: the Continental Congress declined to act on antislavery petitions, and the Declaration's language about liberty coexisted with the protection of slaveholders' interests. But his argument embedded the antislavery cause within the theological framework of covenant and divine judgment—an argument that would resurface with increasing force in the antebellum period.

### Jacob Duché: Chaplain and Loyalist

Jacob Duché (1738–1798) illustrates the religious complexity of 1776. An Anglican minister in Philadelphia, Duché served as the first chaplain of the Continental Congress in 1775–1776, offering the famous opening prayer at the First Continental Congress in September 1774. His prayer for the colonial cause was noted for its eloquence and moved many delegates. Yet by October 1777, Duché—unable to reconcile his Anglican loyalties and his personal uncertainty about independence—wrote a letter to Washington urging him to abandon the revolutionary cause. He subsequently fled to England, returning to America only after the war. Duché's trajectory illustrates the impossible position of American Anglicans in 1776: their church was the established Church of England, headed by the King, yet many American Anglicans were patriots.

### Jonathan Mayhew: Theological Precursor

Jonathan Mayhew (1720–1766) had died a decade before 1776, but his theological influence on the Revolution was profound and still active in that year. His 1750 sermon "A Discourse Concerning Unlimited Submission and Non-Resistance to the Higher Powers" was the first major American theological argument that Christians were not obligated to obey tyrannical rulers—that Romans 13 (the standard biblical text demanding submission to authority) applied to legitimate rulers, not tyrants. John Adams later called Mayhew's discourse "read by everybody," and its arguments circulated throughout the pre-revolutionary generation. By 1776, Mayhew's framework—resistance to tyranny as Christian duty—had become the dominant clerical reading of the Revolution.

## Denominational Divisions

### Anglicans: Caught Between Crown and Colony

The Anglican (Church of England) clergy faced an impossible situation in 1776. Their institutional loyalty ran to the Crown—the King was the head of the Church of England—yet many American Anglican parishioners were ardent patriots. Southern Anglican clergy, less dependent on the Crown than their northern counterparts, tended to support independence more readily; northern Anglican clergy more often remained loyalist or passive. The institutional Anglican church in America effectively collapsed during the Revolution: churches closed, clergy fled or were silenced, and the denominational structure disintegrated. The Protestant Episcopal Church, the American successor to Anglicanism, would reconstitute itself after the war as an independent body.

### Quakers: Conscientious Neutrality

Philadelphia Quakers, concentrated in Pennsylvania's political and commercial elite, were largely committed to pacifism and refused to participate in the war on either side. Their neutrality was interpreted by patriots as loyalism and subjected many Quakers to social pressure, property seizure, and in some cases exile. The [Pennsylvania Constitution of 1776](/news/politics/pennsylvania-constitution-1776/)—one of the most radical of the revolutionary state constitutions—was drafted largely by non-Quaker radicals, in part because Quaker pacifism had removed them from the revolutionary coalition.

### Presbyterians and Congregationalists: The "Presbyterian Rebellion"

British observers sometimes called the Revolution a "Presbyterian rebellion," reflecting the dominant role of Calvinist clergy—Presbyterians and Congregationalists—in providing theological legitimacy for independence. This characterization overstated denominational uniformity but captured a real pattern: the Reformed tradition's emphasis on covenant, corporate responsibility, and resistance to arbitrary authority aligned naturally with revolutionary politics. By 1776, the New England Congregationalist ministry was nearly unanimously patriot; the Presbyterian clergy of the mid-Atlantic and South were similarly aligned.

## Religion, Liberty, and the Unresolved Contradictions

The Declaration's claim that rights were endowed by a "Creator" raised an immediate problem: whose creator? The 1776 colonial population included not only Calvinist Protestants but also Catholics, Anglicans, Quakers, Lutherans, Dutch Reformed, Jews, deists, and non-believers. The Revolution's religious language presumed a broadly Protestant Christian framework while claiming universal applicability.

[Thomas Paine's](/news/people/thomas-paine/) *Common Sense* (January 1776) took a notably different approach: Paine was a deist with little sympathy for institutional Christianity, and his revolutionary argument was grounded in natural reason rather than scriptural authority. Paine's challenge to monarchy explicitly attacked the biblical precedent for kingship (1 Samuel 8), but his deism put him at the margins of clerical respectability. The Revolution thus encompassed both Witherspoon's Calvinist providentialism and Paine's rationalist deism—a theological breadth that foreshadowed the First Amendment's separation of church and state.

The [Pennsylvania Constitution of 1776](/news/politics/pennsylvania-constitution-1776/) was among the first to guarantee religious freedom explicitly: it required belief in God and the divine inspiration of the Old and New Testaments as qualifications for office, but beyond that allowed freedom of conscience for all Pennsylvanians. This compromise—broad but not universal—typified 1776's approach to religious pluralism.

## Significance

Religion in 1776 was not peripheral to the Revolution—it was central. Clergy legitimized resistance as a moral and spiritual duty; churches provided organizational infrastructure for independence meetings; and the theological conviction that the Declaration appealed to truths "self-evident" and ordained by a "Creator" gave the Revolution a moral transcendence that purely political arguments alone could not provide. John Witherspoon's simultaneous role as theologian and legislator embodied the fusion; Samuel Hopkins's antislavery petition demonstrated how the same theological framework could produce radical moral critiques that the Revolution itself refused to face.

Yet the prominence of religious sanction also meant that the unresolved questions of 1776—slavery, women's rights, religious diversity—became embedded in a Christian-inflected founding narrative that would demand reckoning in subsequent generations. The claim that 1776 was fundamentally a religious revolution—not merely a political one—remains underexamined; this gap obscures how deeply scripture and theology shaped the ideological formation of American independence.
