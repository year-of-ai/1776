## Summary

In 1776, the Spanish Crown controlled one of the world's largest empires — a territorial expanse stretching from the Caribbean and Mexico through Central America to South America and the Philippines, encompassing roughly 13 million square kilometers and a colonial population of approximately 15 million. Spain's colonial policy in this year reflected a deliberate strategy of consolidation and expansion on its own imperial terms, largely independent of the American Revolution unfolding in the north. Charles III, reigning since 1759, had pursued the Bourbon Reforms — a systematic program of enlightened administrative modernization — intended to centralize royal control, increase colonial revenue, and strengthen imperial defenses in the aftermath of Spain's humiliating losses in the Seven Years' War (1756–1763).

José de Gálvez, Visitor-General of New Spain (1765–1771) and Minister of the Indies from 1776, aggressively implemented these policies. Gálvez reduced creole influence in colonial governance by replacing American-born officials with peninsulares (Spain-born administrators), tightened Madrid's grip on colonial trade and taxation, and reorganized military forces into a standing colonial army supplemented by militia. Annual revenue from the Spanish American colonies increased from approximately 5 million pesos in the 1760s to over 20 million pesos by the early 1780s as a direct result of Bourbon Reform fiscal measures.

## The Founding of San Francisco

A signal moment of Spanish imperial expansion came on September 17, 1776, when the Presidio Real de San Francisco was formally dedicated on San Francisco Bay; its companion Franciscan mission, Misión San Francisco de Asís (soon known as Mission Dolores), was dedicated on October 9, 1776. The settlement resulted from the overland expedition led by Captain Juan Bautista de Anza, who in 1775–1776 had marched more than 240 colonists from Sonora, Mexico, through the Sonoran Desert and over the mountains to Alta California — a logistical feat covering roughly 1,200 miles.

This founding anchored Spain's northern frontier in Alta California, representing the Crown's strategic decision to extend colonization northward against Russian fur-trading interests expanding south from Alaska and to secure territorial claims in a region that the American Revolution would later bring into geopolitical contention. The [full account of San Francisco's founding]({% post_url politics/1776-09-17-founding-of-san-francisco-1776 %}) details Anza's expedition and the mission-presidio system that structured Spanish California. Father Junípero Serra oversaw the mission network that provided the religious and agricultural backbone of Spanish Alta California; by the end of 1776 the Franciscans had founded the first six of the eventual twenty-one Alta California missions, from San Diego (1769) to San Francisco.

## Bourbon Reforms and Imperial Reorganization

The Bourbon Reforms represented the most thoroughgoing reorganization of Spanish colonial administration since the 16th century. Key measures implemented or consolidated in the 1770s included:

- **Intendancy system**: New Spain was reorganized into intendancies (provincial administrative units modeled on French practice), replacing the older corregidor system and placing professional administrators directly responsible to the Crown in charge of taxation, justice, and public works.
- **Free trade decrees**: The *Reglamento de Comercio Libre* (1778) expanded on earlier partial liberalization measures, opening colonial ports to direct trade with multiple Spanish ports rather than requiring all commerce to flow through Cádiz — a change that increased trade volume significantly but also raised colonial expectations for autonomy.
- **Military reorganization**: Spain established a standing colonial army (ejército permanente) in New Spain during the 1760s–1770s, supplemented by disciplined militia units trained by Spanish officers. By 1776, New Spain's garrison numbered roughly 6,000 regular troops, with militia forces several times larger.
- **Expulsion of the Jesuits**: In 1767, Charles III expelled the Society of Jesus from all Spanish dominions, ending their control of frontier missions in California, the Río de la Plata, Paraguay, and elsewhere. This left mission networks understaffed and created administrative vacuums quickly filled by Franciscans under Serra and Dominicans.

## Spain and the American Revolution

Spain observed the American Revolution with a mixture of strategic interest and ideological anxiety. Interest stemmed from the opportunity to recover territories lost in the 1763 Treaty of Paris — particularly Florida and Gibraltar — by exploiting British military overextension. Anxiety stemmed from the dangerous precedent of colonial independence: if British colonists could successfully revolt against their mother country, what deterred Spain's far larger and more diverse colonial population from attempting the same?

Spain's initial posture was covert assistance rather than open alliance. Even before declaring war on Britain in June 1779, Spain channeled financial and material aid to the American revolutionaries through the fictional trading company Gardoqui and Sons, and through coordination with France. Louisiana's colonial governor Bernardo de Gálvez (nephew of José de Gálvez) proved an aggressive operator: in 1779–1781 he led campaigns that captured British-held Baton Rouge (September 1779), Mobile (March 1780), and Pensacola (May 1781), recovering West Florida for Spain and demonstrating that the American Revolution had ignited a wider imperial war throughout North America.

Spain's declaration of war on Britain in June 1779 — following the April 1779 Treaty of Aranjuez with France — aligned the Bourbon powers against Britain but notably did not include recognition of American independence. Spain sought British territories, not a precedent for colonial self-rule. This distinction shaped Spanish policy throughout the war and into the peace negotiations: Spain's 1783 gains (East and West Florida, Minorca) came at the cost of failing to recover Gibraltar and accepting an independent United States as a potentially destabilizing neighbor on its northern border.

## Comparison with the British Imperial Approach

Where the [British Empire in 1776]({% post_url politics/1776-01-01-british-empire-1776 %}) was grappling with the crisis of parliamentary sovereignty and colonial representation — a constitutional crisis the Prohibitory Act and the Declaration of Independence both addressed — Spain's imperial crisis in 1776 was primarily administrative and fiscal rather than constitutional. Spanish colonists had no representative assemblies comparable to Virginia's House of Burgesses or Massachusetts's General Court; Bourbon Reform centralization actually reduced the limited creole self-governance that had previously existed. The absence of representative institutions meant that colonial grievances accumulated without political channels, contributing to the explosive independence movements that erupted across Spanish America between 1810 and 1826.

## Significance

Spain's 1776 colonial policy established territorial and institutional frameworks that would endure into the early American republic and shape the independence movements of the 1810s–1820s. The founding of San Francisco exemplified Spain's commitment to imperial expansion even as European attention fixed on American independence; Bernardo de Gálvez's campaigns demonstrated that the Revolution had ignited a wider Atlantic war.

More broadly, Spain's cautious observation of the American Revolution — exploiting British weakness without endorsing colonial self-rule — reflects the fundamental tension of Bourbon imperial policy: modernizing and rationalizing the empire in order to preserve and strengthen it, rather than reforming it toward greater colonial autonomy. That strategy postponed rather than prevented the collapse of Spanish American empire, which arrived within a generation of the Bourbon Reforms' apparent success.
