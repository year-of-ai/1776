## Summary

Joshua Reynolds (1723–1792) was the preeminent British portrait painter of the eighteenth century and the founding president of the Royal Academy of Arts, established in 1768. By 1776, Reynolds had consolidated his position as the arbiter of British artistic taste and the most sought-after portraitist among the nobility, gentry, and emerging merchant class. That year he continued his prolific output, completing commissions for aristocratic patrons, political figures, and cultural luminaries during a year of social and political turbulence brought on by the American Revolutionary War.

As president of the Royal Academy and organiser of its annual Summer Exhibition (first held in 1769), Reynolds shaped the institutional framework for British art education and professional practice. His *Discourses on Art*, delivered annually to Academy students — seven had been published by the mid-1770s — articulated a systematic aesthetic philosophy that elevated history painting above portraiture, yet his own commercial success derived entirely from portrait commissions. This paradox defined Reynolds' career: a theorist who preached that portraiture was inferior to history painting, yet a practitioner whose portraits established new standards for the genre.

## Reynolds in 1776: Studio, Sitters, and Exhibitions

Reynolds' studio in Leicester Fields, London, operated on an industrial scale by 1776. He employed specialist assistants — including painters trained in drapery and still-life — to complete background elements while he focused on faces and hands, the elements that commanded his personal attention and the sitter's time. Contemporary accounts suggest he could accept sittings of no more than an hour and a half, producing extraordinary likeness in brief sessions. His fee for a full-length portrait by this period had risen to 200 guineas, a sum that placed him beyond the reach of all but the wealthiest patrons.

The Royal Academy's 1776 Summer Exhibition opened in April at Somerset House and displayed works by Reynolds alongside contemporaries including Thomas Gainsborough, Benjamin West, and Angelica Kauffmann. Reynolds' contributions that year, typical of his practice, included portraits of prominent men and women rendered in the Grand Manner — a style drawing on the postures and settings of Old Master paintings to dignify sitters above their everyday social station.

Among Reynolds' closest intellectual companions in 1776 were the literary critic Samuel Johnson and statesman-philosopher Edmund Burke, both members of the famous Literary Club that Reynolds had helped found in 1764. Johnson's portrait by Reynolds from this period remains one of the most celebrated English literary portraits of the century. Burke, then a Member of Parliament wrestling with the American crisis, was also a subject Reynolds painted multiple times; the portraits created a visual record of the political upheaval that Burke articulated in his oratory.

## The Discourses and Academic Theory

Between 1769 and 1790, Reynolds delivered fifteen *Discourses on Art* to Royal Academy students; seven had been completed by 1776. In these lectures Reynolds synthesized the academic tradition inherited from continental Europe — particularly the French Academy and the theories of Charles Le Brun — with English empiricist philosophy. His central argument was that great art required the generalisation of nature: the painter should not reproduce any individual specimen but synthesise ideal forms from many observed examples, following the example of Raphael and the Antique.

This theory placed Reynolds in direct dialogue with the aesthetic philosophy of Edmund Burke (whose *Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful* appeared in 1757) and with the broader intellectual currents of the [Scottish Enlightenment](/news/arts/scottish-enlightenment-1776/). The tension between Reynolds' academic idealism and the empiricist tradition — which demanded that art be grounded in direct observation — animated British aesthetic debates throughout the 1770s and 1780s.

Reynolds was also a fierce partisan in the rivalry between painting schools. He famously slighted Thomas Gainsborough's looser, more spontaneous style in his Discourses, though he privately acknowledged Gainsborough's genius and delivered a celebrated eulogy for him at the Academy in 1788 following his rival's death.

## Royal Patronage and Cultural Politics

Reynolds had been appointed Principal Painter to the King in 1784, but his relationship with George III was uneasy long before that formal appointment. The King preferred the more deferential and courtly style of Benjamin West, an American-born painter who became a royal favourite. Reynolds found his most enthusiastic aristocratic patrons outside court circles — the great Whig families, literary figures, and members of the educated gentry who prized his blend of social elegance and intellectual depth.

In 1776, the political crisis of the American Revolution directly affected Reynolds' patronage world. Several of his most prominent sitters held positions of military or political responsibility in the conflict — including military officers sent to suppress the American rebellion and politicians debating colonial policy at Westminster. Reynolds' portraits of these men created a visual archive of British authority at a moment when that authority was being fundamentally challenged. His portrait of [King George III](/news/people/king-george-iii-1776/) from this period illustrates the intersection of royal image-making and imperial crisis.

## Competition and Legacy

Reynolds' dominance was not unchallenged. Thomas Gainsborough, based in Bath until his move to London in 1774, and George Romney, who had returned from Rome in 1775 to set up a rival studio in Cavendish Square, offered fashionable alternatives to Reynolds' more cerebral approach. Romney in particular attracted aristocratic and literary sitters who found Reynolds' Grand Manner pretentious or his prices prohibitive. The presence of Angelica Kauffmann and Mary Moser as founding female members of the Royal Academy also complicated Reynolds' cultural authority, representing a distinct approach to history painting and portraiture that drew on continental training and neoclassical subject matter.

By 1776 Reynolds' vision had achieved what might be called institutional capture: the Royal Academy's curriculum, exhibition practices, and prize structure all reflected his theoretical priorities. This created a lasting framework for British art education that persisted well into the nineteenth century, shaping generations of painters who came to prominence after Reynolds' death in 1792.

## Significance

Reynolds' prominence in 1776 epitomized the role of visual culture in a moment of political and social crisis. Even as the American Revolution unfolded and Britain faced military reversals, Reynolds' portrait practice and the Royal Academy's institutional work continued unabated, demonstrating the persistence of aristocratic patronage networks. His portraits of politicians, military officers, and literary figures — figures who appear throughout the record of 1776 alongside accounts like [the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire](/news/arts/decline-and-fall-roman-empire/) — created a visual archive of British power and cultural authority precisely when that authority faced its greatest challenge since the Civil War.

Reynolds' theoretical writings established the academic tradition that would dominate European art education into the twentieth century. His insistence on the moral and intellectual basis of art — derived from classical precedent and contemporary aesthetic philosophy — provided a framework that distinguished "high" art from craft and commerce. This framework enabled him and the Academy to claim authority over artistic standards at a moment when British culture itself was being redefined by Revolution and Enlightenment.

His institutional role as Academy president also positioned Reynolds as a cultural diplomat: the direction of exhibitions and educational programs represented Britain's artistic leadership in Europe during a period when British political and military leadership faced serious questions. The Academy's 1776 Summer Exhibition, for instance, attracted continental visitors and helped establish London as a rival to Paris in European artistic life, an ambition Reynolds pursued with full awareness of its political resonance.
