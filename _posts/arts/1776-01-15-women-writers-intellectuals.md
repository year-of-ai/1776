## Summary

While the American Revolution of 1776 is celebrated as a triumph of male Enlightenment thinkers and patriotic orators, women writers and intellectuals simultaneously engaged in the revolutionary discourse, channeling ideas about liberty, reason, and social change through poetry, letters, published essays, and literary salons. **Abigail Adams**' March 31, 1776 letter to her husband John, urging him to "Remember the Ladies," stands as the best-known example, yet it was one articulation among many female voices. **Elizabeth Graeme Ferguson**, a Philadelphia-based writer and salon host, used her domestic literary circle to discuss revolutionary politics and Enlightenment philosophy with both men and women. **Mercy Otis Warren**, already established as a satirical playwright before the war, authored political plays in 1776 that dramatized the conflict and women's precarious position within it. **Hannah Adams**, though primarily known for historical compilation, was beginning in 1776 to establish herself as a female author in a male-dominated field. Even **Phillis Wheatley**, the enslaved African American poet whose 1776 ode to Washington was published in the [*Pennsylvania Magazine*](/news/arts/pennsylvania-magazine/), asserted intellectual and political voice through verse despite her bondage.

These women's contributions to 1776 reveal a paradox central to the American founding: the Revolution proclaimed universal natural rights and condemned "enslaving" tyranny, yet women—who composed half the population—were denied citizenship, property rights, and formal political voice. Female writers thus worked within and around these constraints, using genres traditionally deemed acceptable (domestic letters, poetry, salon conversation) to insert themselves into debates about sovereignty, liberty, and the shape of the new nation. Their intellectual participation was real and sophisticated, yet systematically erased from the official narrative.

## Key Figures and Their 1776 Contributions

### Abigail Adams and the "Remember the Ladies" Letter

On March 31, 1776, while John Adams was in Philadelphia working toward independence, Abigail Adams wrote her most celebrated letter from Braintree, Massachusetts: "I desire you would Remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors. Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands." John Adams replied dismissively on April 14, calling the idea "saucy." Abigail's letters were not mere personal correspondence—they were a sustained, sophisticated political argument for extending the Revolution's logic to women. Her exchange with Mercy Otis Warren that same spring shows the two women comparing notes on the gap between revolutionary rhetoric and women's actual status. Abigail Adams's full role in 1776 is detailed in her [dedicated profile](/news/people/abigail-adams-1776/).

### Mercy Otis Warren: Political Playwright and Historian

Mercy Otis Warren (1728–1814) had already published two anonymously attributed satirical plays—*The Adulateur* (1772) and *The Defeat* (1773)—lampooning loyalist figures before 1776. In 1776, she continued her political commentary, corresponding extensively with Samuel Adams, John Adams, and other patriot leaders who valued her analysis. Unlike most women writers of the period, Warren circulated her political opinions in semi-public letters that functioned as pamphlets among patriot networks. Her later *History of the Rise, Progress and Termination of the American Revolution* (1805) made her one of the first American historians, male or female, to document the revolutionary period.

### Phillis Wheatley: Verse and Liberty

[Phillis Wheatley](/news/arts/phillis-wheatley-1776/) stands as the most internationally recognized female writer of 1776. Born in West Africa around 1753 and enslaved in Boston, she had published her *Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral* in London in 1773—the first book published by an African American. In 1776, she sent a poem to General George Washington in Cambridge, praising him as defender of liberty; Washington received it warmly and arranged its publication in the *Pennsylvania Magazine* in April 1776. The irony that an enslaved woman was among the most eloquent champions of revolutionary liberty was not lost on contemporary observers, nor on Wheatley herself: her letters reveal a consciousness of the contradiction between slavery and the revolution's stated principles.

### Elizabeth Graeme Ferguson: The Philadelphia Salon

Elizabeth Graeme Ferguson (1737–1801) hosted Philadelphia's most intellectually distinguished literary salon at Graeme Park, her family estate in Montgomery County, Pennsylvania. Her circle—active through the late 1760s and 1770s—included both loyalists and patriots, ministers and physicians, poets and politicians, making it one of the few spaces in colonial America where women directed intellectual conversation on equal footing with men. In 1776, as the political crisis fractured social networks, Ferguson's salon became more complicated: her estranged husband, Henry Ferguson, was a Tory, while she sympathized with the patriot cause. She continued writing poetry and letters that reflected on the war's human cost.

### Judith Sargent Murray: Proto-Feminist Theory

Judith Sargent Murray (1751–1820) was formulating the philosophical arguments she would publish in 1779 as "On the Equality of the Sexes" during the 1776 period. While her landmark essay appeared three years later, the intellectual work of 1776—the readings, the drafts, the exchanges with her first husband John Stevens—anchored her systematic case that women's apparent intellectual inferiority was the product of denied education, not inherent capacity. Murray's approach was grounded in Enlightenment epistemology, arguing that "the minds of women are as capable of improvement as those of men."

## The Paradox of Revolutionary Exclusion

The [Declaration of Independence](/news/politics/declaration-of-independence/) proclaimed "all men are created equal" with "unalienable Rights" to liberty, yet the document's authors excluded women, enslaved people, and Indigenous peoples from its vision. Women writers in 1776 were acutely aware of this contradiction. Abigail Adams framed it explicitly; Phillis Wheatley embodied it; Mercy Otis Warren documented it. The exclusion was not incidental but structural: colonial law treated married women as *femes covert*, legally absorbed into their husbands' identities, unable to own property, sign contracts, or vote. Single women and widows had marginally more legal standing but were still excluded from the political sphere.

Despite this, 1776 created new rhetorical opportunities. The language of natural rights, once deployed, was difficult to contain: if liberty was a natural right, women could ask why they were denied it. The Enlightenment premise that reason was a universal human capacity implied women were as capable of rational political thought as men. Female writers in 1776 exploited these logical openings, even as male Founders consciously refused to follow them.

## Genres and Strategies of Female Intellectual Life

Female writers of 1776 adapted their intellectual expression to the genres available to them:

- **Letters**: The epistolary tradition was culturally acceptable for women and could carry sophisticated political argument. Abigail Adams, Mercy Otis Warren, and Elizabeth Graeme Ferguson all used letters as a semi-public intellectual medium.
- **Poetry**: Poetry—especially devotional and occasional verse—was considered an appropriate female genre. Phillis Wheatley and others used poetic conventions to address political themes obliquely or, in Wheatley's case, directly.
- **Salons and conversation**: The European salon tradition, imported to colonial America, allowed women to host, curate, and direct intellectual conversation without publishing. Ferguson's salon is the best-documented American example.
- **Historical writing and compilation**: Hannah Adams's later career would demonstrate that historical writing was a path available to women. The genre's ostensibly passive role (recording rather than arguing) made it more acceptable than political pamphlets.

## Significance

The presence of women intellectuals in 1776 complicates the narrative of the Revolution as solely a male patriotic achievement. It reveals the existence of an alternative, female-centered intellectual tradition engaging the same Enlightenment texts, debating the same questions of natural rights and political legitimacy, and navigating the tragedy that the Revolution's promise of universal liberty excluded them. The marginalization of these voices—even today—obscures a crucial historical fact: the intellectual foundations of American independence rested partly on female reasoning and female rhetoric, even as political power remained male-monopolized. Moreover, the exclusion of women from the founding generated a debt that subsequent generations of feminists would demand be paid, making 1776's female intellectuals the unacknowledged harbingers of women's-rights movements to come.

The [Scottish Enlightenment](/news/arts/scottish-enlightenment-1776/) texts circulating in 1776—including Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* and David Hume's philosophical writings—shaped the intellectual climate that women writers inhabited and engaged with, even as women were denied formal education in those traditions.
